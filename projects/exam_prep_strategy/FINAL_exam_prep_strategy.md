## Pre-Run Checklist

- [ ] Know the opening line by heart — you should be able to say it without looking at a slide
- [ ] Know the reactivation question exactly: "Raise your hand if you studied hard and still blanked on an exam"
- [ ] Have a visible timer ready for the Verarbeitung phase (phone, projected clock, or online timer)
- [ ] Confirm participants have pen and paper — or tell them to use the back of any handout
- [ ] Know the two misconceptions to watch for: "I already quiz myself" and "I should know it before testing myself"
- [ ] Have one sentence ready to park out-of-scope questions: "Great — that's the next level. Worth exploring separately."
- [ ] Know the closing line for the Assessing phase: "That discomfort is the learning. Use your gaps."
- [ ] If the group is experienced: prepare to acknowledge it after Reactivating and adjust Informing to be confirmatory rather than introductory

## Intended Learning Outcome

By the end of this workshop, participants will be able to:

> **Use** retrieval practice as a study method by recalling course content from memory without looking at notes, and identifying gaps to guide further study.

**Success signal:** A participant has met the ILO if they attempted recall and can identify at least one gap in their knowledge — demonstrating that they have used the strategy, not merely understood it.

**What this workshop does NOT cover:**
- Spaced repetition — the complementary scheduling strategy; requires its own session
- Other high-utility learning strategies (elaborative interrogation, interleaving, practice testing with feedback)
- How to build a full exam study plan or time-management system

## Full Facilitator Script

---

### A — Arrival [0:00–1:00]

**Purpose:** Orient participants. Name the topic and the ILO as a concrete promise. Nothing else.

> **Script:**
> "Hi, I'm [name]. In the next 10 minutes, you're going to use the most effective study technique most students never use — and by the end, you'll have already done it once. Let's go."

> **Cue:** The ILO is delivered as a promise, not a formal statement. It names an action ("you'll have already done it") and creates mild curiosity. Don't explain what it is yet — let the R phase surface the need first.

**Transition:** Move directly into the reactivation question. No pause.

---

### R — Reactivating [1:00–2:30]

**Purpose:** Surface the problem that retrieval practice solves. Almost every student has experienced studying hard and still blanking — this question creates instant common ground.

> **Script:**
> "Quick question before we get into it. Raise your hand if this has ever happened to you: you studied — actually put in real hours — and then sat in the exam and your mind just went blank on something you'd reviewed."
>
> *(Wait. Nearly all hands should go up.)*
>
> "Good. Almost everyone. That experience has a name and, more importantly, a fix. That's what the next 10 minutes are about."

> **Cue:** Look around the room when hands go up. Acknowledge the breadth: "Yeah — most of you." If only a few hands go up, say: "Even if it's only happened once, it's worth knowing why."

**Watch for:** Students who look confused or don't raise their hand — they may be very early in their studies. That's fine; the session is still useful as prevention.

**Transition:** "Here's what's actually happening when that occurs — and what you can do about it."

---

### I — Informing [2:30–7:00]

**Purpose:** Explain the "illusion of knowing" (why re-reading fails) and introduce retrieval practice as the concrete alternative. Use examples, not citations.

> **Script:**
> "Here's what happens when you re-read your notes. Everything looks familiar. You think: 'Yes, I know this.' But here's the thing — familiar is not the same as remembered. Familiar just means you've seen it before.
>
> The moment you close your notes and try to recall something from scratch — no prompts, no text in front of you — that familiar feeling evaporates. Because recognition and recall are not the same skill. And exams test recall.
>
> There's a good analogy here. Imagine you want to get stronger at push-ups. You could watch videos of other people doing push-ups. You'd recognise exactly what the movement looks like. But you wouldn't get stronger. You need to do the reps yourself. Re-reading is watching the video. Retrieval practice is doing the reps.
>
> So what does retrieval practice actually look like? It's simple: you close your notes, you grab a blank sheet of paper, and you write down everything you can remember about a topic. No looking. You will get things wrong. You will have gaps. That is the point — because finding those gaps IS the studying. The struggle to remember, even when you fail, is exactly what builds the memory.
>
> Students who quiz themselves consistently outperform students who re-read, even when the re-readers spend more time studying. The method works. Most students just don't use it."

> **Cue:** The push-up analogy is the turning point — land it clearly before moving to the mechanics. Do not add a second concept. At around [6:30], check in: "Any quick questions before we try it?" Take one, max.

**Transition:** "Now I want you to actually try it — right now."

---

### V — Verarbeitung [7:00–9:30]

**Purpose:** Participants do one complete retrieval practice attempt with their own material. This is the ILO in action.

> **Script:**
> "Here's what we're doing. You need a pen and a blank piece of paper — or the back of whatever you have. Pick one topic you're studying right now. Could be anything. A concept, a chapter, a term.
>
> In a moment I'm going to say go. When I do: close your notes, don't look at your phone, and write down everything you can remember about that topic. Don't worry about gaps. Don't worry about getting it perfect. Just write.
>
> Two minutes. Ready? Go."

> **Cue:** Start a visible timer immediately. Walk quietly around the room. Don't hover. Don't talk.

> **Cue at ~1:30:** "Thirty seconds — wrap up your thought."

**To close the task:**
> "Stop. Put your pen down. Now I want you to do one thing: look at what you wrote and notice what's NOT there. What couldn't you pull up? What's missing? Those gaps — right there — that is your actual study list. You just used retrieval practice."

**Transition:** "Last question before we finish."

---

### A — Assessing [9:30–10:00]

**Purpose:** Close the loop on the ILO. Check that participants experienced finding a gap — not just that they heard about the technique.

> **Script:**
> "Hands up — who just found at least one gap? Something you were surprised you couldn't remember?"
>
> *(Hands go up — most of them.)*
>
> "That feeling of finding a gap — that IS the strategy working. That discomfort is not failure. It's the learning happening. Next time you sit down to study: start there. Close your notes. Write what you remember. Use the gaps.
>
> That's retrieval practice. You've already done it once."

> **Cue:** If almost no hands go up: "Even if you felt like you got most of it, try it tonight with a topic you haven't reviewed recently. The gaps show up faster than you expect." Then close.

---

### Contingency Notes

**If the group finishes Verarbeitung in under 1 minute:**
> Ask them to pick a second topic: "If you feel like you covered that one, pick a second. Or go deeper on the same one — what else do you remember?"

**If the group is slow to start Verarbeitung:**
> Prompt gently: "Any topic at all. What do you have an exam on next? Even if it's just one word — start there." Don't restart the timer; give the 30-second prompt earlier.

**If Verarbeitung runs over:**
> Cut the close-task sequence. Move directly to the show-of-hands Assessing question. Skip the one-volunteer share.

**If a student asks about spaced repetition or other strategies:**
> "Yes — that pairs perfectly with this. That's the next level. For today, we're focused on just this one."

**If the group is very experienced:**
> After Reactivating: "Some of you may already use this. Great — then use the Verarbeitung to go harder: pick a topic that's genuinely difficult and see how it holds up."

## Facilitator Tips

**Tip 1: Let the silence work during the reactivation**
When you ask "raise your hand if you've studied hard and still blanked" — stop talking. The silence is the point. Students need a moment to actually remember the experience before they raise their hand. If you fill the gap immediately, you lose the emotional hook that makes the rest of the session land.

**Tip 2: Don't rescue students during Verarbeitung**
When students are struggling to remember things during the 2-minute task, the impulse is to help. Resist it. The struggle is the learning. Walk the room quietly. If someone looks truly stuck, the only prompt to offer is: "What's the first word that comes to mind about this topic?" Then let them keep going.

**Tip 3: Name the misconception pre-emptively**
Students often push back mentally with "I already do this — I use flashcards sometimes." You can defuse this in the Informing phase by saying: "Some of you might be thinking you already do this. Maybe you use flashcards occasionally. The difference is: doing it systematically, before you feel ready — not just as a final check after you feel confident." This lands better than addressing pushback reactively.

**Tip 4: The close is everything**
The session's emotional payoff is the moment students look at their page and see what's missing. Give that moment space. After you say "notice what's NOT there" — pause for 5 seconds before moving to the Assessing question. Let the gap sink in.

**Tip 5: Adapt the level of experience quickly**
After the reactivation show-of-hands, you have about 3 seconds to read the room. If almost everyone puts their hand up: go at normal pace. If only a few do: slow down the Informing phase and make the push-up analogy even more explicit. If hands suggest a very experienced group: acknowledge it immediately and move faster through Informing.

## If You Want to Go Deeper

*For participants who ask "what next?" after the session.*

- **Make It Stick** by Peter Brown, Henry Roediger, and Mark McDaniel — the most readable book on the science of learning; the chapter on retrieval practice is the place to start.
