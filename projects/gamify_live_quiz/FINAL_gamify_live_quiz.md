## Intended Learning Outcome

By the end of this workshop, participants will be able to:

> **Design a single live-quiz question that connects a game mechanic to a specific learning outcome — so students retrieve and apply knowledge rather than guess.**

**Success signal:** A participant has met the ILO if they can articulate that a well-designed question requires active retrieval or application — not recognition of something stated seconds earlier, and not guessable from context or answer options alone.

**What this workshop does NOT cover:**
- Step-by-step walkthroughs of specific tools (Kahoot!, Mentimeter, Poll Everywhere) — tool choice is secondary to question design
- Designing a multi-question quiz arc across a full lesson — that requires a separate session
- Grading, scoring models, or assessment validity — important but not the entry point
- Adapting gamification for online or asynchronous formats — a different design challenge

## Full Facilitator Script

---

### A — Arrival [0:00–1:00]

**Purpose:** Orient participants immediately. State who you are, what the session covers, and what they will be able to do by the end. Nothing else.

> **Script:**
> "Hi, I'm [name]. In the next 10 minutes we're going to look at **live quizzes and gamification** — specifically, how to design them so they actually check learning, not just participation. By the end, you'll be able to **write one live-quiz question that uses a game mechanic to make students retrieve and apply knowledge** — not just click the first answer that looks right. Let's go."

> **Cue:** Say the ILO clearly and directly. Don't soften it or add caveats. Participants should hear a concrete, achievable promise — one question, well designed — not a vague invitation to "explore gamification."

**Transition:** Move immediately into the reactivation question. No pause, no preamble.

---

### R — Reactivating [1:00–2:30]

**Purpose:** Surface a shared frustration — the participation gap — that participants already live with. This makes the concept relevant before it's introduced.

> **Script:**
> "Before we get into it — think of the last time you asked students a question mid-class and only two or three hands went up. Hands up if that's happened to you."
>
> *(Wait. Almost every hand should go up. Acknowledge it.)*
>
> "Right. Every hand. Here's the thing: that gap — between the number of students in the room and the number willing to answer — is exactly the problem a well-designed live quiz solves. Not by making class more 'fun' — but by changing the social contract of answering."

> **Cue:** If only a few hands go up, add: "How many students are typically in your class? And how many answer voluntarily? That ratio is what we're working with." The reactivation is about making the problem visceral — don't rush past it.

**What to listen for:** Nods, small laughs of recognition. The mood should shift slightly — from polite attention to "yes, this is real."

**Transition:** "Here's what changes when you design the quiz right…"

---

### I — Informing [2:30–7:00]

**Purpose:** Deliver one concept clearly: a live quiz only drives learning when a game mechanic is attached to a question that requires genuine cognitive retrieval. Use examples, not definitions.

**The concept:**
> A game mechanic without a learning outcome is entertainment. A learning outcome without a game mechanic is homework. The live quiz is where they collide — and that collision is what you design.

> **Script:**
> "Here's what most instructors do when they first try Kahoot! or Mentimeter: they import questions from a question bank, or they copy-paste from last semester's test. And students have a good time — there's the countdown, the music, the leaderboard. But if you look at the questions, most of them can be answered by guessing from context, or by remembering what was said two slides ago. The mechanic is there. The learning isn't.
>
> The reason is simple: **game mechanics amplify whatever the question is already doing.** If the question requires retrieval — genuinely making students pull something from memory and connect it — the countdown and the leaderboard turn that retrieval into a high-stakes moment that burns the answer in. If the question just requires recognition — 'which of these four things did I just say?' — the mechanic turns it into a reflex game.
>
> So the design move is this: before you write a quiz question, ask yourself — **'Can a student answer this correctly without having understood anything?'** If yes, rewrite it. A good live-quiz question should be answerable only if the student can retrieve and apply something — connect a term to a context, spot the error in an example, choose the right next step in a scenario.
>
> Quick example. Instead of: *'What is the definition of confirmation bias?'* — ask: *'A researcher only reads studies supporting their hypothesis. What cognitive bias is most likely at play?'* Same concept. The second question requires the student to recognise an instance of the concept in context — that's retrieval plus application. That's what makes the game mechanic earn its place."

> **Cue:** You have 4–5 minutes. One concept, one example, one rewrite. If you notice yourself adding a second idea (e.g. how to choose between tools, or how to score quizzes), stop — park it. The concept must land first.

> **Cue:** Around 6:30, check in: "Does this distinction make sense — between a recognition question and a retrieval question? Any quick questions before you try it?" Take one question max, then move.

**Watch for:** Someone asking "which tool is best?" Say: "Tool choice is secondary — today we're building the question first. The tool is just the delivery mechanism."

**Transition:** "Now I want you to try it."

---

### V — Verarbeitung [7:00–9:30]

**Purpose:** Participants apply the principle immediately to something real. They produce a concrete artifact: one quiz question from their own course.

**The task:**
> Think of one concept from your own course that students regularly get wrong or forget quickly. In pairs, write one quiz question — just one — that uses a game mechanic to check whether they actually understood it. You have **2 minutes**.

> **Script:**
> "Here's what I want you to do. You have **2 minutes**. Turn to the person next to you. Think of one concept from your course that students regularly get wrong or forget. Then write one quiz question — just one — that requires students to retrieve and apply that concept. Not a definition question. A question where the correct answer is only reachable if they understood.
>
> *(Start visible timer — phone, projected clock, or call it out.)*
>
> Go."

> **Cue:** While they work, circulate and listen. You're looking for questions that are still definition-style or recognition-only — these are the examples to gently reframe when you debrief. Note 1–2 strong examples to name-check in the close.

> **Cue:** At 1:30: "Thirty seconds — finish your question."

**To close the task:**
> "Time. Let's hear one — tell me the question you wrote, and tell me: what's the learning outcome it's checking?"
> *(Take 1–2 responses. Listen for whether they can name the outcome. If they can, affirm it — "exactly, that's the connection." If they can't, prompt gently: "What did you want students to be able to DO with that concept?")*

**Transition:** "Last question before we finish."

---

### A — Assessing [9:30–10:00]

**Purpose:** Close the loop on the ILO. One direct question that checks whether participants can articulate the principle — not just report that they enjoyed the activity.

**The question:**
> "What's the difference between a quiz question that checks understanding and one that just checks whether students were paying attention?"

> **Script:**
> "One last question — and I want a one-sentence answer. What's the difference between a quiz question that checks understanding and one that just checks whether students were paying attention?"
>
> *(Collect 2–3 responses. Listen for language around retrieval, application, connecting to context.)*
>
> "Exactly. The question that checks understanding requires the student to do something with the knowledge — not just recognise it. That's the design move. That's what makes the game mechanic worth it.
>
> You've just done 10 minutes on live-quiz design. The question you wrote in pairs — that's the one to try in your next class. Start there."

> **Cue:** If responses are vague ("it's more specific?"), prompt: "What does the student have to *do* with the knowledge to get it right?" That usually surfaces the retrieval principle. Don't re-teach — just redirect.

---

### Contingency Notes

**If the group is too fast (finishes Verarbeitung in under 90 seconds):**
> Ask a second prompt: "Now — could a student answer your question correctly without having understood the concept? How would you close that loophole?"

**If the group is too slow (Verarbeitung runs over):**
> Cut to one show-of-hands assessing question: "Hands up if your question requires retrieval — if a student who just guessed or recognised a keyword couldn't answer it correctly." Then close.

**If someone asks about a specific tool (Kahoot! vs. Mentimeter etc.) during Informing:**
> "Great question — that's about tool choice, which is out of scope today. The principle we're working with applies to any tool. Happy to talk tools after." — Park it, note it, move on.

**If the group is already experienced and designs retrieval questions:**
> After Reactivating: "Sounds like you're ahead of the curve — great. Then our Verarbeitung isn't about learning the principle, it's about sharpening: does your question still have a loophole a clever student could exploit without understanding?"

**If participants are sceptical that gamification is serious pedagogy:**
> Absorb the scepticism during Reactivating without being defensive. Say: "That's exactly the right instinct — entertainment and learning aren't the same thing. The principle we're looking at today is about making them collide deliberately." Move on.

## Facilitator Tips

**Tip 1: Absorb scepticism early — don't fight it**
Instructors who associate gamification with "making things fun" are not wrong — they've seen it misused. Don't defend gamification; instead, validate the scepticism and redirect it: "That's exactly right — entertainment and learning aren't the same thing. What we're doing today is making them collide deliberately." The research does the arguing; you just have to give it room.

**Tip 2: Model the rewrite yourself**
The confirmation bias example in the Informing phase is most powerful when you deliver it conversationally, not read it off the slide. Practice the before/after rewrite out loud until it feels natural. If your audience teaches in a specific discipline, swap in an example from their field — a medical diagnosis, a legal scenario, a design brief. The principle is identical; the example lands harder when it's theirs.

**Tip 3: The timer is a teaching tool, not just logistics**
The visible 2-minute timer in Verarbeitung is part of the pedagogy, not just time management. It mimics the countdown mechanic of the live quiz — participants experience the mild pressure of a game mechanic while designing one. If you can project the timer, do it. If not, announce "90 seconds left" and "30 seconds" explicitly. Don't let the task drift open-ended.

**Tip 4: Name the distinction, don't just feel it**
During the Verarbeitung debrief, some pairs will produce excellent retrieval questions without being able to say why they're good. Help them name it: "You've written a question where the correct answer is only reachable if the student understood — not guessed, not remembered what you said 30 seconds ago. That's the distinction." Naming it out loud is what converts experience into transferable principle.

**Tip 5: Don't let tool questions hijack Informing**
In a room of instructors who've heard of Kahoot! and Mentimeter, someone will ask "which tool is best?" This is a genuine and reasonable question — but answering it opens a rabbit hole that burns the Informing phase. Park it warmly: "Tool choice is secondary — today we build the question first. After the session I'm happy to compare tools. What are you currently using?" Write it down visibly. Return to it only if time allows.

## If You Want to Go Deeper

*For participants who ask "what next?" after the session.*

**Retrieval Practice (The Learning Scientists)** — learningscientists.org/retrieval-practice. A concise, evidence-based overview of why retrieval beats re-reading and passive review, with classroom implementation guides. Free, peer-reviewed, directly applicable.

**"Make It Stick" by Brown, Roediger & McDaniel** — The most accessible book-length treatment of retrieval practice, spaced repetition, and interleaving for educators. Chapter 2 ("To Learn, Retrieve") is the most directly relevant for this workshop's concept and takes under an hour to read.
